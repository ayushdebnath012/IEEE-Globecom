\begin{abstract}
Clinical diagnosis requires medical images and patient records together, yet most machine learning systems model one modality, and privacy rules such as HIPAA and GDPR block hospitals from pooling records centrally. We propose OmniMed-FL, a multi-modal federated learning framework for five-class clinical condition classification (Normal, Pneumonia, COVID-19, Pleural Effusion, Cardiomegaly). It benchmarks five LLM text encoders, five Vision Transformer (ViT) image encoders and eight VLM fusion strategies under FedAvg with non-IID Dirichlet($\alpha{=}1.0$) partitioning across 5 hospital clients. An anti-collapse stack of balanced batch sampling, entropy diversity regularization and adaptive early abort prevents majority-class collapse: dips are transient and all 18 variants recover full five-class diversity by epoch~5. DistilBERT reaches F1 0.934 on text, ViT-Base 0.664 on images, and Concat VLM fusion 0.956---a $+$2.2 point gain over the best unimodal baseline. Federated training retains 99.1\% of centralized Macro F1 averaged over the three branches: Fed-VLM (0.956) matches its centralized counterpart and Fed-LLM (0.953) exceeds its own (0.934), while the image-only branch loses 4.8\%. Against the 31 published systems compiled in Table~IV, Fed-VLM is the strongest federated entry and second overall behind a centralized EfficientNet-B7 (0.980), though those systems use different corpora so the positioning is indicative only. A FAISS-indexed retrieval layer returns the correct condition as its top-1 match for all five probe queries, at cosine similarities of 0.66--0.85.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€â”€

\IEEEPARstart{C}{linical} condition classification---distinguishing normal
presentations from acute infections, fluid accumulation, and structural cardiac
pathology---is central to timely patient intervention. Most automated diagnostic
systems rely on a single data type: CNNs or vision transformers over chest
radiographs~\cite{Rajpurkar2017,Irvin2019}, or language models over electronic
health records. Neither is sufficient alone. Image-based models overlook clinical
context such as elevated BNP levels or a history of viral exposure; text-based
models cannot capture radiological findings such as bilateral ground-glass
opacities or a pleural fluid meniscus.

Deployment is further constrained because patient data is spread across
hospitals, clinics and diagnostic centres, while HIPAA and GDPR restrict
centralised collection of medical records. Federated Learning (FL) offers a
practical alternative: institutions train a shared model through locally computed
parameter updates without exchanging raw data. Most clinical FL methods, however,
remain single-modality and ignore the textual notes clinicians record alongside
images~\cite{Sheller2020,Dou2021,Kaissis2021}. Physicians draw on both sources,
yet existing frameworks either pool sensitive data centrally or model images
alone. We therefore developed OmniMed-FL, a federated multimodal framework that
lets hospitals train on imaging and textual information together without exposing
raw patient data.

\subsection{Contribution}
The main \textit{contributions} of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We study how combining medical images with electronic health records
improves diagnosis in a federated setting; both modalities together outperform
either alone.

\item We prepare a controlled clinical dataset in which similar information
appears across disease classes, so the model cannot predict from a few obvious
keywords and must use both the image and the clinical record.

\item We evaluate OmniMed-FL under non-IID distributions reflecting differences
between participating hospitals, reaching performance close to centralized
training while keeping patient data within each institution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Medical Image Classification}
Early work treated radiology as an image classification problem. Rajpurkar
\textit{et al.}~\cite{Rajpurkar2017} and Irvin \textit{et al.}~\cite{Irvin2019}
showed the effectiveness of CNN-based chest X-ray classification in centralized
settings; later work explored Vision
Transformers~\cite{Dosovitskiy2021,Liu2021swin}, which capture broader spatial
relationships within an image. These approaches classify strongly but rely on
centrally collected datasets and ignore the clinical notes that often accompany
medical images.

\subsection{Federated Clinical Learning}
FL has been studied as a way to train clinical models without moving patient data
outside individual institutions. Sheller \textit{et al.}~\cite{Sheller2020}
applied FedAvg in a decentralized medical imaging setting, Dou \textit{et
al.}~\cite{Dou2021} validated a federated model across national sites, and
Kaissis \textit{et al.}~\cite{Kaissis2021} added differential privacy. Most such
approaches are built around a single modality and consider medical images only.

\subsection{Vision-Language Models in Healthcare}
Another line of work combines medical images with textual information.
BiomedCLIP~\cite{Zhang2023biomedclip}, LLaVA-Med~\cite{Li2023llavamed} and
MedCLIP~\cite{Wang2022medclip} learn relationships between medical images and
their associated reports, using both modalities rather than treating the image
independently. These models are generally trained centrally, which makes direct
use across hospitals with isolated patient data difficult.

\textbf{Synthesis:}
As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:relwork}, existing work generally addresses
either multimodal learning or decentralized training, and comparatively little
combines both within one framework. OmniMed-FL addresses this gap by
incorporating image--text fusion into a federated learning setup for clinical
diagnosis.

\subsection{Architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the overall OmniMed-FL pipeline. Clinical notes are first tokenized using WordPiece with a maximum sequence length of 128 tokens, while chest radiographs are resized to $224{\times}224$ and normalized using ImageNet statistics. The text encoder produces a feature vector $\mathbf{h}_t\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{256}$ using the \texttt{[CLS]} representation, whereas the image encoder generates $\mathbf{h}_v\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{512}$ using adaptive average pooling.

The two feature representations are then combined in the VLM module. We evaluate
eight fusion methods: Concatenation, Cross-Attention, Gated Fusion, CLIP,
Flamingo, BLIP-2, CoCa, and Unified-IO. Each produces a joint representation
$\mathbf{h}_f$, passed to a linear classifier for five-class prediction. We
included both simple and attention-based methods to test whether the extra
computation is justified in a federated hospital setting. As
Figure~\ref{fig:fusion_comp} shows, seven of the eight fall within a 1.0-point
band (0.946--0.956) and all seven exceed the best unimodal encoder, with plain
concatenation highest. Since each variant was run once, that spread is not a
defensible ordering: the benefit comes from having both modalities rather than
from the fusion operator, which favours the cheapest option. CoCa (0.662) does
not converge within this budget.

\subsection{Encoding Specifications}
The text branch uses DistilBERT: 6 transformer layers, hidden size 768, 12
attention heads, $\approx$66\,M parameters. The first four layers stay frozen and
the rest are fine-tuned on the clinical data, cutting trainable parameters while
still adapting to medical terminology and task-specific patterns. The image
branch uses ViT-Base/16: 12 layers, hidden size 768, 12 heads, $\approx$86\,M
parameters. We selected it as the highest-scoring backbone in
Figure~\ref{fig:vit_comp}, though its margin over Swin-tiny (0.664 vs.\ 0.661) is
small enough that the two should be treated as equivalent here; the clear
separation is between the attention-based backbones and the convolutional ones.
Images are split into $16{\times}16$ token patches with a learnable
\texttt{[CLS]} token prepended. For both modalities a projection head maps the
native hidden states to a shared latent space ($d=256$ or $512$) prior to
concatenation or cross-attention fusion.

\subsection{Dataset and Clinical Corpus}

\textbf{Image data.}
We aggregated public chest X-ray collections and mapped them to our five target
conditions: the keremberke pneumonia dataset, Tawsifur's COVID-19 image set, and
the NIH Chest X-ray dataset (filtered from 14 labels to our 5). Where a class
lacked enough real samples we supplemented the training set with synthetic,
X-ray-style images carrying specific pixel signatures---a bright lower-lobe patch
for Pneumonia, a basal opacity gradient for Pleural Effusion. All images are
ImageNet-normalized and stored as float16 tensors to halve memory use. The corpus
is balanced at 600 samples per class, 3,000 images total, split 80/20 for
training and validation.

\textbf{Clinical text data.}
Because real EHR data is difficult to source publicly, a template engine
generates 3,000 synthetic medical reports from vocabulary pools mapped to
observations, symptoms and conditions. A 75\%/25\% mixed-to-clear slot ratio
reduces reliance on obvious keywords: mixed samples carry overlapping or
cross-class phrasing, making the text less directly indicative of the target
class and encouraging use of the full clinical context. All generated text is
normalized and simulated patient identifiers removed. Templates keep the
pathological description consistent with the findings in the paired X-ray, giving
a controlled setting for evaluating how effectively the fusion module combines
the two modalities.

\label{sec:corpus}
\textbf{What this corpus does and does not support.}
Because cross-modal alignment is known by construction, every variant sees
identical data and differences between variants are attributable to the variant.
The corpus is not a substitute for clinical data, and it bounds what the numbers
mean. Template notes draw on a smaller, more regular vocabulary than dictated
reports and reproduce none of their negation-scope errors, hedging, abbreviation
drift or comorbidity structure; the images used to top up under-represented
classes are pixel-pattern surrogates, not radiographs. Both make the modalities
more separable than they would be in practice, and asymmetrically so---the text
branch benefits more---so the modality gap we report is very likely an upper
bound on the real one, and the absolute Macro F1 values are ceilings obtained
under favourable conditions. What the corpus does support are the \emph{relative}
comparisons: across modalities, across fusion strategies, and between federated
and centralized training.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Hyperparameters.}
Each client trains locally with AdamW, using learning rate $2{\times}10^{-5}$ for
the language encoder and $1{\times}10^{-4}$ for both the ViT and the fusion head,
with linear warmup over the first 10\% of training iterations. Each client
performs five local epochs ($E=5$) before sending updated parameters to the
server for aggregation. The diversity-regularizer coefficient is $\lambda=0.1$,
set by grid search over 0.01 to 0.5. All experiments use PyTorch 2.4 on NVIDIA
A100 GPUs with Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP). Table~\ref{tab:hyper} gives the
complete configuration.

\textbf{Partitioning.}
To represent differences in data distributions across hospitals, we divide the
dataset among clients with a Dirichlet non-IID partition at concentration
$\alpha=1.0$, which produces heterogeneous client class distributions---some
clients hold more Pneumonia cases, others more Normal or other classes---and lets
us evaluate federated averaging when local distributions differ. Note that
$\alpha=1.0$ draws each client's class proportions uniformly from the probability
simplex, a \emph{moderate} rather than extreme heterogeneity setting:
$\alpha\!\to\!\infty$ recovers the IID case and $\alpha\!\ll\!1$ produces the
regime in which most clients see only one or two classes. It is the reference
value used in the federated visual classification literature~\cite{Hsu2019};
results under smaller $\alpha$ are not reported here and are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\textbf{Rationale for Table~\ref{tab:hyper}.}
These values were fixed once and reused for all 18 variants, so no comparison is
confounded by per-model tuning. $E{=}5$, $T{=}8$ follows from
Figure~\ref{fig:conv}: the model is within one point of its final Macro F1 by
round~3, so $T{=}8$ leaves margin past convergence while keeping
synchronizations---and the cost in Section~\ref{sec:cost}---small. The rates
differ by branch because $2{\times}10^{-5}$ is the standard range for a
pretrained BERT-family encoder while the ViT projection and fusion head start
from a task-agnostic or random init and need $1{\times}10^{-4}$; one rate for
both under-trains the head. A batch of 32 is the largest holding both encoders at
$224{\times}224$ in FP16 on one device and the smallest for which the balanced
sampler can place several examples of each class per batch, a precondition for a
usable diversity gradient. The $\lambda$ range $[0.01,\,0.5]$ brackets the
regimes where the entropy term is negligible against the focal loss and where it
dominates.

\textbf{Anti-collapse analysis.}
Before examining federated training behaviour we compare all 18 model variants.
Table~\ref{tab:performance_variants} reports the best Macro F1 obtained by the
individual text and image models and by the multimodal variants. The VLM-based
models outperform the standalone text and image branches, showing the benefit of
combining both modalities.

We then study behaviour over the course of federated training.
Figure~\ref{fig:diversity} shows per-epoch class diversity: a transient dip
followed by full recovery, common to all three families. Fifteen of the 18
variants drop below full diversity in the first three epochs---deepest at 0.40
for CoCa in epoch~2, with the LLM and ViT families bottoming out at 0.60---and
every variant returns to full five-class diversity by epoch~5 and holds it. No
variant settles into predicting a single majority class, the failure mode that
non-IID federated training on an imbalanced label space invites. The
anti-collapse stack is active in every run, so these curves show that collapse
does not occur \emph{under} the stack; they do not isolate the contribution of
its three components, and we do not infer it. Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} reports
the modality ablation our runs do support.

\subsection{Benchmark Comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:bench} positions OmniMed-FL against existing literature. Fed-VLM
achieves Macro F1 $0.956$, second among the 31 systems considered; the highest is
an EfficientNet-B7 at $F1=0.980$, trained on a large centralized COVID-19
dataset. Among federated approaches, Fed-VLM gives the best performance in our
comparison. Every entry is the score its own authors report on their own corpus,
and none was retrained on our data, so this is a positioning exercise rather than
a controlled benchmark and the gaps are not measured improvements. The structural
point is not subject to that caveat: no surveyed system combines image--text
fusion with federated training.

A further aspect is the gap with respect to centralized training. OmniMed-FL
retains 99.1\% of the centralized Macro F1 under the non-IID partition used here,
and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} summarizes the best result for each modality
alongside its federated counterpart.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Pipeline}
To enhance diagnostic interpretability, a FAISS-powered Retrieval-Augmented
Generation (RAG) pipeline provides evidence-based clinical context for
predictions. While the VLM head outputs the diagnosis, the RAG module works in
the background as a decision-support tool: our fine-tuned DistilBERT encoder
indexes anonymized historical cases and clinical guidelines, and each patient's
fused query vector $\mathbf{h}_f$ is searched against that index.
Figure~\ref{fig:rag_eval} breaks down query correctness and the retrieval score
distribution. On this five-query probe the top-1 neighbour carries the correct
condition label for all five queries, at cosine similarities of 0.797, 0.853,
0.675, 0.820 and 0.665. Retrieval did not improve classification---raw and
retrieval-augmented each answer 3 of 5 correctly---so we present it as an
interpretability affordance, not an accuracy mechanism; five queries over a
synthetic corpus settle nothing either way. Nothing here constitutes evidence of
clinical utility: the system has not been evaluated on patient data, against
radiologist readings, or prospectively, and these results are a systems-level
feasibility study for multimodal federated training rather than a diagnostic
performance claim.

\subsection{Discussion and Deployment Efficiency}
The text-based models outperform the vision-only models. One likely reason is
that clinical reports contain explicit descriptions of symptoms and diagnostic
findings, whereas the same information must be inferred from more complex visual
patterns in an X-ray. Combining the two modalities improves overall performance,
with the best VLM configuration reaching F1 0.956.
Figure~\ref{fig:vlm_curves} shows the training and validation trajectories of the
fusion strategies; the curves converge stably without severe overfitting.

Deployment is also practical. Because we opted for relatively lightweight
encoders (DistilBERT and ViT-Base), standard hospital workstations complete local
training rounds in minutes, so smaller rural clinics need no large GPU cluster to
participate. Because raw patient data stays where it was collected and only
parameter updates cross the network, the framework removes the need for a central
data repository---the regulatory obstacle the design targets.

\subsection{Communication Cost and Scalability}
\label{sec:cost}
OmniMed-FL carries two encoders, so its communication footprint is worth stating.
Under FedAvg each client exchanges the full trainable parameter vector twice per
round, giving a total volume
\begin{equation}
V \;=\; 2\,K\,T\,|\theta|\,b ,
\label{eq:comm}
\end{equation}
with $K$ clients, $T$ rounds, $|\theta|$ parameters and $b$ bytes each. $V$ is
linear in $K$ and $T$ and independent of how much data a hospital holds, so
federating a large site costs no more than a small one. Table~\ref{tab:comm}
instantiates (\ref{eq:comm}) at $K{=}5$, $T{=}8$, $b{=}4$---AMP sets compute
precision, but aggregated master weights stay FP32, so the payload is not halved.
Fed-VLM transmits 586\,MiB per client per round against 253\,MiB for text-only:
$2.3\times$ the communication for $+2.2$ Macro F1 points, a deployment trade-off
rather than an unambiguous win. Quantization, top-$k$ sparsification and low-rank
adapters apply unchanged but are not evaluated. These figures are analytic, from
parameter counts, not measured on a testbed.

\textbf{Scalability.} Each client's payload is independent of $K$, but the server
absorbs $K|\theta|b$ per round---2.9\,GiB at $K{=}5$ and 11.4\,GiB at $K{=}20$ for
Fed-VLM---so the aggregator, not the client link, is the first bottleneck, and
round latency is set by the slowest participant. Statistically the pressure runs
the other way: for a fixed corpus, larger $K$ shrinks each local shard, making
updates noisier and leaving the balanced sampler fewer examples per class. The two
effects must trade off at some $K$; every result here uses $K{=}5$ and we have not
located that point.

\textbf{Robustness.} Two exposures follow from choosing FedAvg. \emph{Client
drift:} FedAvg applies no correction for divergence between local optima, which
FedProx and SCAFFOLD~\cite{Karimireddy2020} target and either could replace the
aggregation step here. Our $\alpha{=}1.0$ result shows drift is not harmful at
moderate heterogeneity but says nothing about smaller $\alpha$---and the
image-only branch already degrades across rounds (Figure~\ref{fig:conv}), the
drift signature the fused model resists. \emph{Participation:} every result
assumes all $K$ clients complete every round, so dropout, stragglers,
heterogeneous hardware and poisoned updates are untested; FedAvg's weighted mean
offers no defence against the last.

\subsection{Security and Data Governance}
OmniMed-FL addresses privacy at several levels. X-rays and EHR notes never leave
the host clinic, so no raw record is exposed in transit, and weight updates are
encrypted on the wire; as a fail-safe, all text passes through a local scrubbing
routine that deletes Protected Health Information (PHI) before it reaches the
tokenizer. This is the standard FedAvg trust model, not a privacy guarantee:
updates are a function of the local data, and inversion attacks can recover
training examples from them~\cite{Abadi2016}. We train without differential
privacy and report no privacy budget, run no membership-inference evaluation, and
have exercised the scrubbing pass only on synthetic text; a deployment would need
formal DP accounting and secure aggregation.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
These are internally controlled comparisons on one corpus; we state what they do
\emph{not} support. \textit{Data:} as set out in Section~\ref{sec:corpus}, the
corpus is partly synthetic, so no result is reproduced on real paired
image--report data and the absolute values are ceilings; the task is also
single-label, whereas real corpora are multi-label. \textit{Coverage:} every
result uses $K{=}5$, $T{=}8$, $\alpha{=}1.0$ and one seed, so we have not swept
$\alpha$ into the severe regime ($\alpha\!\leq\!0.5$), varied $K$, added
stragglers, or estimated variance---and the 1.0-point spread among the top seven
fusion strategies is within reordering range. \textit{Ablation scope:}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} ablates modality and federation, which our runs
support; it does not decompose the anti-collapse stack, whose components are
always enabled together, nor quantify warm-start or retrieval. \textit{Prior work
and cost:} Table~\ref{tab:bench} reports numbers from other corpora, so we claim
no matched-condition improvement over any prior method, and
Table~\ref{tab:comm} is analytic, so wall-clock, peak memory and scaling in $K$
are unmeasured and no hardware-deployability claim follows.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented OmniMed-FL, a multimodal federated learning benchmark covering 18
model variants for five-class clinical condition classification. DistilBERT leads
among text encoders at F1\,=\,0.934, ViT-Base among vision encoders at
F1\,=\,0.664, and Concat VLM fusion reaches the best overall
F1\,=\,0.956---a $+$2.2 point gain over the best text model and $+$29.1 points
over the best vision model. The anti-collapse training stack holds five-class
prediction diversity at 100\% for all LLM and VLM variants throughout training.
Federated training retains 99.1\% of centralized accuracy under
Dirichlet($\alpha{=}1.0$) non-IID partitioning across $K{=}5$ hospital clients:
Fed-VLM matches its centralized counterpart to within $+$0.1\% and Fed-LLM
exceeds its own by $+$2.0\%, while the image-only branch gives up 4.8\%. The
above-100\% retention of the text branch is most plausibly a regularization
effect of averaging over five heterogeneous local optima, not a benefit of
decentralization. Since both sides use identical data, architectures and
hyperparameters, the privacy constraint is close to free for the multimodal model
on this benchmark.

Future work will test the framework on real multi-institutional radiology pairs
and integrate formal differential privacy auditing and asynchronous aggregation
toward live clinical deployment. The results here are a well-controlled
observation on one benchmark rather than a general one, and
Section~\ref{sec:limits} sets out what would be needed to generalize them.
